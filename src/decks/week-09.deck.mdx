## Compliance is not agreement

Kelman (1958) distinguishes three ways a person can come to follow a rule:

- **Compliance**: to gain a reward or avoid a sanction, while privately disagreeing
- **Identification**: to maintain a relationship with the person or group who holds the rule
- **Internalisation**: because the rule matches the person's own values

Only the first stops when surveillance stops.

```notes
Kelman, H. C. (1958). Compliance, identification, and internalization. Journal of Conflict Resolution, 2(1), 51–60. See also Cialdini & Goldstein (2004), Annual Review of Psychology 55, for the later literature on compliance and conformity. The useful question for any course policy is which of these three it relies on. A rule enforced only by a mark of 0 is asking for compliance, and it will get exactly that.
```

---

## Obedience: Milgram (1963)

- 26 of 40 participants administered the maximum 450-volt "shock" when told to continue by an experimenter
- Obedience fell as the learner was brought closer, and as the authority was moved further away
- Milgram's account: the **agentic state**, in which a person sees themselves as the instrument of another's wishes

Later work is more cautious. Replications (Burger, 2009) reproduce the effect at lower voltages; archival work (Perry, 2012) questions how standardised the procedure was.

```notes
Milgram, S. (1963). Behavioral study of obedience. Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 67(4), 371–378. Burger, J. M. (2009). Replicating Milgram. American Psychologist, 64(1), 1–11. Perry, G. (2012). Behind the Shock Machine. Haslam and Reicher (2012, PLoS Biology) reframe the results as "engaged followership": participants continued when they identified with the experimenter's scientific purpose, not simply because they were told to. That reframing matters for institutions. People comply most readily with an authority whose purpose they share.
```

---

## Conformity: Asch (1951, 1956)

- Participants judged which of three lines matched a reference line, after hearing a unanimous group give a wrong answer
- About a third of responses on critical trials conformed to the group; most participants conformed at least once
- A single dissenting ally reduced conformity sharply

The finding most relevant to a course is the last one. Unanimity is what produces conformity, and one voice breaks it.

```notes
Asch, S. E. (1956). Studies of independence and conformity: A minority of one against a unanimous majority. Psychological Monographs, 70(9). In a lecture theatre, the unanimous group is everyone who does not ask the question. The student who asks whether a rule is consistent with the class summary is the dissenting ally for everyone else in the room.
```

## Learned helplessness

- Seligman and Maier (1967): animals exposed to shocks they could not control later failed to escape shocks they could control
- Maier and Seligman (2016) revise the account: passivity is the default response to prolonged adversity, and what is learned is **control**

An institution that refuses every request does not only refuse requests. It teaches students that requesting is futile, and they stop.

```notes
Seligman, M. E. P., & Maier, S. F. (1967). Failure to escape traumatic shock. Journal of Experimental Psychology, 74(1), 1–9. Maier, S. F., & Seligman, M. E. P. (2016). Learned helplessness at fifty: Insights from neuroscience. Psychological Review, 123(4), 349–367. Compare this with the extension data in any course: a low request rate is often read as a sign that students do not need extensions. It is equally consistent with students having learned that requests fail.
```

## Weber: authority that rests on rules

Weber (1922) describes **rational-legal authority**: a rule is obeyed because it was enacted by correct procedure, not because of who issued it or whether it is good.

- Its strength is impersonality: the same rule for everyone
- Its risk is the "iron cage" (*stahlhartes Gehäuse*): a system of rules that no one inside it can step outside of, including the people who administer it

```notes
Weber, M. (1922/1978). Economy and Society. (It is on this course's prescribed list.) The "iron cage" is Parsons's 1930 translation of a phrase from The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1905). The point for a university: a convener who says "the policy does not allow it" is invoking rational-legal authority. The question worth asking is whether the procedure that enacted the policy was itself followed. That is what the class summary procedure is for.
```

## Foucault: the examination

Foucault (1975) identifies three instruments of disciplinary power:

- **Hierarchical observation**: being visible to those who judge you
- **Normalising judgement**: being measured against a norm, and corrected towards it
- **The examination**: which combines the two, and turns each person into a case

Discipline works best when it is internalised: the observed begin to observe themselves.

```notes
Foucault, M. (1975/1977). Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the Prison (trans. A. Sheridan), Part III. The panopticon is the image most people remember, but the examination is the more useful concept here. A requirement to commit to version control every hour is an examination that never ends: the student is always visible, always measured, and eventually does the measuring themselves.
```

## Arendt: thoughtlessness

Arendt's account of Eichmann (1963) proposed that great harm can be done by functionaries who are not especially malicious, but who do not **think**: who do not stop to judge what the rule in front of them is for.

Arendt's claim is not that every official is dangerous. It is that following procedure is not, by itself, a moral act.

```notes
Arendt, H. (1963). Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil. The phrase is often misread as "anyone could be evil". Arendt's later work (The Life of the Mind, 1978) makes the positive claim: thinking, the habit of stopping to examine what one is doing, is what conditions people against doing harm. The scale of a university's harms is not comparable to her subject, and the lecture does not suggest it is. The mechanism is what transfers: "I was following the policy" is a description, not a justification.
```

## When the measure becomes the target

- Goodhart (1975): a statistical regularity collapses once it is used for control
- Campbell (1979): the more a quantitative indicator is used for decisions, the more it corrupts the process it measures
- Strathern (1997): "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"

A course that assesses compliance with its rules will receive compliance with its rules. It will not necessarily receive learning.

```notes
Goodhart, C. (1975). Problems of monetary management: The U.K. experience. Campbell, D. T. (1979). Assessing the impact of planned social change. Evaluation and Program Planning, 2(1), 67–90. Strathern, M. (1997). 'Improving ratings': Audit in the British university system. European Review, 5(3), 305–321. Muller's The Tyranny of Metrics (2018) is on the prescribed list and extends the argument to universities directly. Scott's Seeing Like a State (1998) makes the related point that institutions simplify what they govern in order to make it legible, and then govern the simplification.
```

## Exit, voice and loyalty

Hirschman (1970): when an organisation fails its members, they have two responses.

- **Exit**: leave (withdraw before census)
- **Voice**: stay and argue for change, individually or together

Loyalty delays exit and makes voice more likely. Voice is more effective when it is collective.

For discussion: when is compliance the rational choice, and when is it voice?

```notes
Hirschman, A. O. (1970). Exit, Voice, and Loyalty: Responses to Decline in Firms, Organizations, and States. Students enrolled in a compulsory course have limited exit, which, on Hirschman's account, should make voice more important, not less. The census date is an exit point. Learning outcome 9 (organising collectively) is about voice.
```
